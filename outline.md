# THE MANY WAYS OF THE SACRED
## A Global History of Religion from the Paleolithic to the Present
### Detailed Book Outline — 50 Chapters + Appendices (~1,040 pages)

---

## I. The Book as a Whole: Design, Argument, and Architecture

**The governing argument.** Religion is not a single "thing" that has a birth date and a progress narrative. It is a family of practices — involving sacred power, ritual action, authoritative narratives, communities of memory, and answers to suffering and death — that has been continuously reinvented in response to new technologies of power (agriculture, writing, empire, print, capital, the digital network). The book therefore resists two temptations: the *theological* one (religion as slow ascent to true belief) and the *skeptical* one (religion as fading illusion of the pre-scientific mind). It treats religion as a set of human solutions to persistent human problems, and it tracks how those solutions changed when their context changed.

**Method.** Interdisciplinary: historical-critical scholarship, archaeology, epigraphy, art history, anthropology, sociology, cognitive science, and (with care) the theology of the traditions themselves. Every major claim is anchored in primary evidence — inscriptions, texts in translation, excavated objects, ritual manuals, court records, hagiographies — and every interpretive controversy is named rather than smoothed away.

### Introduction: What Is Religion? Definitions, Methods, and the Problem of Origins (pp. 1–20)

**Purpose.** To give the reader a working definition, a map of the discipline, and the intellectual tools used in all fifty chapters.

1. **The word "religion" and its history** — Latin *religio* (Cicero's "re-reading," Lactantius's "binding"); the absence of a native equivalent in Sanskrit *dharma*, Arabic *dīn*, Chinese *jiào/zōngjiào*, Japanese *shūkyō* (a Meiji-era coinage), Hebrew *dat* (a Persian loanword). "Religion" as a term of comparison and as a colonial export.
2. **Six working definitions tested against evidence**
 - *Substantive* (Tylor: belief in spiritual beings; Frazer: magic–religion–science).
 - *Functional* (Durkheim: the sacred/profane distinction and social cohesion; Geertz: a system of symbols establishing moods and motivations; Tillich: "ultimate concern").
 - *Family resemblance* (Wittgenstein; Benson Saler).
 - *Practice-first* (Bell, Asad: discipline, ritual, embodied habit).
 - *Cognitive* (Boyer, Atran: agency detection, minimally counterintuitive concepts, costly signaling).
 - *Indigenous/vernacular* categories (the *navel* of the argument: when does a local "way" become a "religion"?).
3. **The comparative problem** — the "dilemma of the polythetic definition"; the risk of "theology by other means"; the difference between *comparison as explanation* and *comparison as analogy*.
4. **The "origins" problem** — three bad stories (fall from a primordial monotheism; ascent from magic to science; the "psychological need" story) and one better one (religion as adaptive repertoire with multiple, convergent origins).
5. **Tools the reader will use** — the axial-age hypothesis, the "social formation" model (elite/literate/popular), the "religious marketplace," the concept of *syncretism* and its critics, the "great tradition / little tradition" model (Redfield) and its limitations.
6. **Case exercise: reading one ritual two ways** — a Balinese temple festival (Geertz) and a contemporary Pentecostal healing service, read with the same six questions: *What is at stake? Who has authority? What bodies do? What is said/texted? What changes? What is remembered?*

**Features.** Box: "Ten Books That Made the Field" (Müller, Weber, Durkheim, Otto, Eliade, Geertz, Talalad, Asad, Bellah, Boyer). Table: "Chronology of the Discipline of Religious Studies."

# PART I — ORIGINS: RELIGION BEFORE HISTORY (pp. 21–132)

*Framing problem: how do we study the sacred in the absence of written testimony? Six chapters move from methodology through deep cognitive history to the first ritual architecture and the earliest literate civilizations.*

---

### Chapter 1 — Before the Word: Evidence, Anachronism, and the Craft of Ancient Religious History (pp. 25–42)

**Thesis.** The prehistoric historian of religion works with a permanently degraded archive; methodological humility is not a hedge but a discipline.

1. **The evidence problem** — material culture (architecture, burials, deposits), iconography, ethnographic analogy, ancient DNA and isotopes; the "ethnographic record is not a time machine" warning.
2. **Three anachronisms to avoid** — projecting "belief" backward (do prehistoric people "believe" or "do"?); projecting "religion" as separable from "life"; projecting denominations (Paleolithic "shamanism," Neolithic "goddess worship").
3. **Interpreting a burial** — a worked example: double burial at Sungir (c. 34,000 BCE, red ochre, 5,000 mammoth ivory beads) — status? afterlife belief? mourning? All three? None?
4. **The politics of prehistoric religion** — the uses of "prehistoric matriarchy" in nineteenth- and twentieth-century movements; contemporary Pagan and Goddess appropriations; who owns the past?
5. **A code of practice for the ancient chapters** — separating *demonstrable* from *probable* from *possible* from *speculative* claims; how the book will mark these.

**Key terms:** *ethnoarchaeology*, *tertiary analogy*, *horizon vs. tradition*, *parsimony*, *Wesleyan "levels of confidence."*

---

### Chapter 2 — Deep History: Mortality, Mind, and the Cognitive Roots of the Sacred (pp. 43–60)

**Thesis.** Human religious capacity is a byproduct of cognitive evolution — hyperactive agency detection, theory of mind, ritualized action, and the capacity for symbolic thought — activated by the single fact of mortality.

1. **The mortuary record** — Qafzeh and Skhül (c. 100,000 BCE), Shanidar (the "flower burial" and its long controversy), Amud, Teshik-Tash; Sunghir (c. 34,000 BCE) and the Dolní Věstonice triple burial.
2. **The cognitive toolkit** — HADD (Barrett), "minimally counterintuitive concepts" (Boyer), the "by-product" debate vs. adaptationist accounts (Sosis & Alcorta's costly signaling; Henrich's cultural-group selection).
3. **The shamanism debate** — Eliade's *Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy* (1951); the neuropsychological model (Lewis-Williams & Dowson); the shattering critique (Henri-Paul Prins, Sven Ouzman, Tim Ingold): is "shamanism" a Siberian concept wrongly universalized?
4. **Upper Paleolithic image-making** — Chauvet (c. 36,000 BCE), Lascaux (c. 17,000 BCE), Altamira, the "deep cave" problem; acoustic properties of caves; Clottes & Lewis-Williams's "shamanic" reading and its critics (White, Conkey).
5. **Music, dance, trance, and the "effervescent" body** — the archaeology of instruments; the pharmacological evidence (ergot, psilocybin debates).

**Object in Focus:** The Lion-Man of Hohlenstein-Stadel (c. 40,000 BCE) — 31 cm mammoth ivory, Ulm Museum. What does an anthropomorphic therianthrope tell us about the capacity for counterintuitive beings?

---

### Chapter 3 — The Neolithic Revolution: Agriculture, Ancestors, and the First Temples (pp. 61–78)

**Thesis.** The domestication of plants and animals produced a new religious problem — how to anchor a settled community in place through ancestors, fertility, and the memory of the dead — and its solution was monumental ritual.

1. **Sedentism and ritual before farming** — Jericho (tower c. 8000 BCE, plastered skulls), 'Ain Ghazal (c. 7200 BCE, lime-plaster statues, caches), Nahal Hemar cave.
2. **Göbekli Tepe (c. 9600–8200 BCE)** — the enclosure complexes; Klaus Schmidt's "first cathedral" hypothesis; the "cult then crops" thesis and its critics (Dietrich, Notroff); what the absence of domestic architecture means.
3. **Çatalhöyük (c. 7100–5700 BCE)** — houses, sub-floor burials, "shrine" rooms, the leopards, the vultures; Mellaart's "mother goddess" reading vs. Hodder's reflexive re-excavation (*Reading Çatalhöyük*); the "Neolithic of the Fertile Crescent" and the invention of storage.
4. **The Goddess question** — Marija Gimbutas's "Old Europe" and the *kurgan* invasion thesis; critiques (Ruth Tringham, Lynn Meskell, Talalay); why the Goddess hypothesis is simultaneously a feminist recovery and an interpretive overreach.
5. **Ancestors, grain, and priesthood** — the emergence of ritual specialists, the "big man" economy, feasting as religious technology; mortality, fertility, and the control of surplus.

**Key Concept:** *Domestication of the sacred* — the shift from mobile ritual specialists to place-bound, property-holding cult centers.

---

### Chapter 4 — Cities of the Gods: Mesopotamia and the Ancient Near East to 1200 BCE (pp. 79–96)

**Thesis.** In the first cities, the temple *was* the economy; divination, lament, and cosmic order were technologies of governance.

1. **Uruk and the temple economy (c. 3500–3000 BCE)** — the White Temple, the Anu ziggurat, the *en* and *lugal*, ration lists, the temple as redistributive warehouse.
2. **Priesthoods and specialists** — the *bārû* (diviner), *āšipu* (exorcist), *asû* (physician), *kalû* (lamentation priest), *nadītu* (cloistered women of the Shamash temple at Sippar); the correspondence of Esarhaddon's court scholars.
3. **Myth and cosmic order** — *Enuma Elish* (Marduk, the *me*, the split of Tiamat), *Atrahasis* (the flood and the "noise" of humanity), *Inanna/Ishtar's Descent*, the *Erra Epic*; *Gilgamesh* — Enkidu, the cedar forest, Ishtar's rejection, Utnapishtim, the plant of immortality lost to the serpent (a striking parallel with Genesis 3).
4. **Ethics and piety** — law codes (Ur-Nammu c. 2100 BCE; Hammurabi c. 1754 BCE) as divine legitimation; "I Will Praise the Lord of Wisdom" (*Ludlul bēl nēmeqi*) — the "Babylonian Job" and the problem of innocent suffering; the *Šurpu* and *Maqlû* incantation texts (witchcraft and slander).
5. **Collapse and continuity** — the Late Bronze Age collapse (c. 1200 BCE), the "Sea Peoples," and the survival of Mesopotamian motifs into Israel, Greece, and the biblical canon.

**Text in Focus:** *Gilgamesh* Tablet X (the tavern-keeper Siduri's counsel: "let your belly be full… dance and be merry") — hedonism, despair, and the ancient answer to mortality.

---

### Chapter 5 — Ma'at and the Beautiful West: Religion in Ancient Egypt (pp. 97–114)

**Thesis.** Egyptian religion was a technology of cosmic maintenance: the pharaoh sustained *ma'at* (truth/order) against *isfet* (chaos), and the afterlife was engineered by ritual and text.

1. **Ma'at and kingship** — the *ka*, the *ba*, the royal titulary, the Sed festival; the "Egyptian theology" problem (Assmann's concept of *Ma'at* as the "cosmic constitution").
2. **The mortuary complex** — Pyramid Texts (Unas, c. 2350 BCE), Coffin Texts, the *Book of the Dead*, the Amduat and the night journey of Ra; the judgment of the heart against the feather of Ma'at (Spell 125).
3. **The Osiris complex** — death, resurrection, and the democratization of the afterlife (c. 2000 BCE onward); Isis, Horus, Set, and the "Isiac" religion's expansion across the Greco-Roman world (Apuleius's *Metamorphoses* XI).
4. **The Amarna interlude (c. 1353–1336 BCE)** — Akhenaten, the Aten, the *Great Hymn to the Aten*, the abandonment of Amarna; Was it monotheism, monolatry, henotheism, or a political theology of the sun? The counter-argument (Assmann's "Mosaic distinction").
5. **Temple, festival, and daily life** — Karnak and the Opet festival; Deir el-Medina (the workers' village and its magical practice); the *Coffin Text* "spells of transformation"; animal cults (Apis, Bubastis, ibis at Saqqara).
6. **"Heka" and the religion/magic distinction** — why the Egyptians had no word for "magic" as opposed to "religion."

**Key Concept:** *Cosmic maintenance* — the notion that ritual does not commemorate the world but keeps it in being.

---

### Chapter 6 — Indus, Steppe, and the Indo-European Horizon (pp. 115–132)

**Thesis.** Two of the most consequential religious streams of Eurasia — the Indus Valley civilization and the Indo-European-speaking pastoralists — are known mostly by inference; the chapter is therefore as much about the limits of knowledge as about belief.

1. **Harappa and Mohenjo-daro (c. 2600–1900 BCE)** — the Great Bath, the "granary," the fire altars at Kalibangan and Lothal, the "proto-Shiva" seal (anachronistic reading), the pipal-tree motif, the unicorn seal, the script's undecipherability (1,500+ texts, no Rosetta Stone).
2. **What can be responsibly said** — ritual bathing, seated meditation posture, symbolic animals, a cult of fire and water; what *cannot* be said (there is no demonstrable Indus "religion").
3. **The Indo-European reconstruction** — the comparative method; Dumezil's trifunctional ideology (sovereignty, force, fertility); \*Dyēus ph₂tēr → Dyaus Pitar, Zeus, Jupiter, Tyr; the horse sacrifice (*aśvamedha*), the Vedic *ṛta*, the Norse *Ragnarök*; the critiques (Lincoln, Littleton, the "Chicago school").
4. **The steppe question** — chariots, the Sintashta culture (c. 2100–1800 BCE), the horse and the ideology of war-ritual; the Aryan migration into South Asia c. 2000–1500 BCE and the current state of the genetics debate (Narasimhan et al.).
5. **A charged controversy handled honestly** — the "Aryan Invasion Theory" vs "Out of India" dispute; the politics of historiography in contemporary India; the difference between a scholarly debate and a national myth.

**Debate Box:** *Do genetics settle historical questions?* — what ancient DNA can and cannot prove about "peoples" and "religions."

# PART II — THE AXIAL AGE, c. 800–200 BCE (pp. 133–280)

*Framing problem: a genuinely global pivot. In several unconnected regions, sages and prophets began to question the ritual order, to speak of a hidden moral structure of the cosmos, and to found durable communities of transformation. Was this coincidence, or a common cause?*

---

### Chapter 7 — The Axial Age: Idea, Evidence, and Its Discontents (pp. 137–154)

**Thesis.** The axial-age hypothesis is the best available large-scale frame for the mid-first millennium BCE — provided it is used as a heuristic, not as a law of history.

1. **Jaspers and the origin of the idea** — *The Origin and Goal of History* (1949); the "simultaneous" breakthroughs in China, India, Iran, Israel, Greece; the "spiritual situation of the age."
2. **Eisenstadt's "axial civilizations"** — the institutionalization of transcendence; the "tension" between the transcendental and the mundane order; Bellah's *Religion in Human Evolution* (2011): the "age of embedded cultures" to "theoretical cultures."
3. **What actually happened c. 800–200 BCE** — the spread of iron, the rise of coinage (Lydia c. 600 BCE), literacy, market towns, the "shatter zone" of the Neo-Assyrian and Persian empires; the "secondary state formation" model.
4. **Four critiques** — the correlation problem (why not 500 years earlier?); the missing regions (Mesoamerica, sub-Saharan Africa); the "high religion" bias (the axial age devalues practice, myth, and body); the "prophetic" over-emphasis (why privilege critique over continuity?).
5. **A defensible reformulation** — "the age of empires and their margins," in which certain kinds of reflexive critique became available where three conditions met: literate record-keeping, urban anonymity, and a nearby empire's coercive reach.
6. **Continuity and rupture** — what the axial sages *kept* from the old order (sacrifice, ancestor, caste, ritual purity) and what they abandoned.

**Debate Box:** *Was the Buddha an axial thinker or an Indian renouncer in a much older line?* (Dumont vs. Gombrich vs. Collins.)

---

### Chapter 8 — Fire and Word: The Vedic World and the Upanishadic Turn (pp. 155–172)

**Thesis.** The Vedic tradition moved from a religion of sacrificial technique to a religion of interior knowledge — a shift that defined the whole subsequent history of South Asia.

1. **The Rigveda (c. 1500–1200 BCE)** — the ten books; Indra and the dragon Vṛtra; Agni as "priest of the sacrifice"; Varuṇa and *ṛta*; the *Soma* hymns (RV 9); Uṣas (Dawn) and the aesthetics of the hymn.
2. **The ritual system** — the *yajña* as cosmic machinery; the *Brāhmaṇa* literature (*Śatapatha*, *Aitareya*); the *Śulba Sūtras* and the geometry of the fire altar; the cosmic man *Puruṣa* (RV 10.90) and the origin of the *varṇas*.
3. **The Upaniṣadic turn (c. 800–200 BCE)** — *Bṛhadāraṇyaka* and *Chāndogya*; *tat tvam asi* ("thou art that"), the identity of *ātman* and *Brahman*; *karma*, *saṃsāra*, *mokṣa*; the *Kena*, *Kaṭha*, *Īśā*, *Muṇḍaka*, *Śvetāśvatara*; the "Upanishads as a genre of conversation."
4. **Householder and renouncer** — Dumont's argument; the *āśrama* system; the *sannyāsin* and the "death in life"; the Vedic-to-Shramanic transition.
5. **Alternatives to orthopraxy** — the Cārvāka/Lokāyata materialists (the "deniers of the Veda"), the Ājīvika determinists (Makkhali Gosāla), the *Yogas* and the *Yoga Sūtras* of Patañjali (c. 200 BCE–200 CE).
6. **The Jains and the Buddha as a shared "second urbanization"** — what the *śramaṇa* movements share (renunciation, non-violence, karma, monasticism) and what they dispute.

**Text in Focus:** *Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad* 3.2 (Yājñavalkya and Maitreyī: "It is not for the sake of the husband that the husband is dear, but for the sake of the Self") — a feminist and a philosophical reading.

---

### Chapter 9 — The Buddha and the Sangha (pp. 173–190)

**Thesis.** The historical Buddha left no writings; what was preserved was a monastic community whose discipline was itself the vehicle of doctrine.

1. **The problem of sources** — the Pāli Nikāyas vs. the Āgamas (Chinese parallels); the "Historical Buddha" debate (Gombrich, Schumann, Collins, Anālayo); the archaeological turn (the Lumbini pillar inscription of Aśoka, c. 249 BCE; Tilaurakot; the Piprāhwā relics debate).
2. **The core teaching** — the Four Noble Truths as a "medical" structure (diagnosis, etiology, prognosis, prescription); *paṭiccasamuppāda* (dependent origination); the three marks (*dukkha*, *anicca*, *anattā*); the Middle Way; the *Satipaṭṭhāna* and the origins of *vipassanā*.
3. **The Sangha** — the *Vinaya* (Pātimokkha), the 227 rules of the *bhikkhu*, the *bhikkhunī* order and the disputed eight "heavy rules" (*garudhammā*); *upāsaka/upāsikā* and the lay-monastic exchange of *dāna* for *dhamma*.
4. **Aśoka (c. 268–232 BCE) and the Mauryan moment** — the Rock and Pillar Edicts, the "conquest by *dhamma*," the Third Council legend, the "mission" accounts (Mahinda to Sri Lanka, the *Mahāvaṃsa*), the *avabhṛtha* massacre critique.
5. **Early schisms** — the *Mahāsaṅghika* vs. *Sthavira* split at Vaiśālī; the "five hundred" at Pāṭaliputra; the problem of "original Buddhism" reconstruction; the *Kathāvatthu*.
6. **What early Buddhists did** — pilgrimage (the eight great sites), relic stupas, the *Jātaka* tales, the *kathina* robe-offering, the role of the *upāsaka* donor in inscriptions at Bharhut, Sanchi (c. 100 BCE).

---

### Chapter 10 — Jainism and the Discipline of Non-Harm (pp. 191–208)

**Thesis.** Jainism is the world's most rigorous experiment in the ethics of the body, and its long survival in merchant communities shows how a religion of strict discipline can be socially sustainable.

1. **History and sources** — Mahāvīra (Vardhamāna), traditional dates 599–527 BCE (vs. the critical 540–468 BCE); the 24 *Tīrthaṅkaras*; Pārśvanātha and the question of what Mahāvīra innovated.
2. **Doctrine** — *ahiṃsā* (non-harm), *anekāntavāda* (many-sidedness) and *syādvāda* (conditional predication); karma as *pudgala* (material) adhering to the *jīva*; the *guṇasthānas*; *sallekhanā* (ritual fasting to death) and its modern legal controversy in India.
3. **Community** — the *Digambara* ("sky-clad") vs. *Śvetāmbara* ("white-clad") division; the role of the *śrāvaka* laity; the "five great vows"; the *sthanakavasi* and *terāpanthi* reform movements (18th c.).
4. **Material culture** — Ranakpur, Dilwara (Mount Abu), the colossal *Bāhubali* at Śravaṇa Beḷagoḷa (c. 981 CE), the *patta* painting tradition, the annual *paryuṣaṇa* festival and the *samvatsari* confession.
5. **Jain ethics in the modern world** — Gandhi (1869–1948) and Jain influences (Shrimad Rajchandra, Raychandbhai); the Jain "animal welfare" hospitals; the vegetarian economy; the global Jain diaspora and the "digital *darśana*."
6. **Comparison box** — Jainism and Buddhism: two renouncer solutions to the same problem of *karma*; why one is monastic-biased and the other world-facing.

---

### Chapter 11 — Prophets, Kings, and Covenant: Ancient Israel and the Invention of Monotheism (pp. 209–226)

**Thesis.** Israelite religion did not begin as monotheism; it arrived there through crisis, exile, and the scribal class that survived it.

1. **Sources and method** — the Documentary Hypothesis (Wellhausen 1878), the supplementary and fragmentary models (Rendtorff, Van Seters); "minimalists" (Thompson, Lemche, Davies) vs. "maximalists" (Dever, Hoffmeier); the archaeological record (Merneptah Stele c. 1207 BCE, Khirbet Qeiyafa, the Tel Dan inscription, the Ebal altar debate).
2. **The religion of early Israel** — covenant forms, the *bamot* (high places), the household cult, Asherah and the "Yahweh and his consort" inscription from Khirbet el-Qom/Kuntillet 'Ajrud; the *elohim* and the divine council (Psalm 82).
3. **The emergence of monotheism** — monolatry vs. henotheism vs. monotheism; the Josianic reform (621 BCE) and the "Book of the Law" found in the Temple (2 Kings 22–23); Deuteronomy and the centralization of worship.
4. **The prophets** — Amos ("let justice roll down"), Hosea and the marriage metaphor, First Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Second Isaiah ("I am the Lord, and there is no other"); the "prophetic critique of sacrifice" and the "ethical monotheism" claim (Sharpes, Smith) — and its critics.
5. **Exile and transformation** — the fall of Jerusalem 586 BCE; Psalm 137; the "death of the temple" and the birth of the synagogue; Ezra-Nehemiah, the Torah as portable homeland; the Second Temple and the rise of apocalyptic (Daniel, 1 Enoch, Jubilees).
6. **The "Mosaic distinction" debate** — Jan Assmann's thesis that monotheism introduced an unprecedented intolerance of "false gods"; responses (Baden, Schwartz, Guy Stroumsa); what changed in the ethics of religious violence.

**Text in Focus:** Deuteronomy 6:4–9 (*Shema*) and Psalm 137 — the grammar of exclusive loyalty and the grammar of trauma.

---

### Chapter 12 — Asho Zarathushtra and the Iranian Religious Imagination (pp. 227–244)

**Thesis.** Zoroastrianism bequeathed to Judaism, Christianity, and Islam the architecture of the afterlife, the drama of cosmic dualism, and the expectation of a final renovation.

1. **The dating problem** — the traditional date (c. 1500–1000 BCE) vs. the linguistic/archaeological argument (c. 1200–600 BCE) vs. the "late Zoroaster" (7th–6th c. BCE) hypothesis; the Gathas as the only secure source.
2. **The Gathas** — the five great hymns of Zarathushtra; Ahura Mazdā, the six *Aməša Spəntas* (Good Mind, Righteousness, Holy Devotion, Perfect Sovereignty, Health, Immortality), the twin spirits (Spəṇta Mainyu vs. Angra Mainyu, *Yasna* 30).
3. **The moral cosmos** — *asha* (truth/order) vs. *druj* (lie); the freedom of human choice; the *Chinvat* bridge and the three-night judgment; heaven (*Garōdmānā*), hell (*Duzakh*); the *Saoshyant*, the *Frashō.kərəti* (renovation), and the resurrection of the dead.
4. **Institutional history** — the *magi*; the Achaemenids (Darius and the Behistun inscription, the question of Ahuramazda in Persian religion); the Parthians and the Sasanians (224–651 CE), the *Avesta*'s compilation under Khosrow I; *zurvanism*; fire temples, the *bareshnum* purification, and the funerary towers of silence.
5. **Diffusion and influence** — the "Zoroastrian" elements in late Judaism (angels, demons, resurrection, final judgment), Christian eschatology, Islamic *fitra*, the *shayṭān*; the "Persian origin" debate (Bousset, Widengren).
6. **Zoroastrians today** — the Parsis (arrival in Gujarat c. 8th–10th c., the *Qissa-i Sanjan*), the Iranis, the Atash Behram temples, the *Navjote*; the "conversion" controversy (intermarriage and the definitional boundaries of the community), and the exodus from post-1979 Iran.

---

### Chapter 13 — Greece: Gods of the City, Philosophers of the Cosmos (pp. 245–262)

**Thesis.** Greek "religion" was not a set of doctrines but a civic practice; Greek "philosophy" began by criticizing that practice and ended by producing new cults of its own.

1. **The polis religion model** — Robert Parker's *Athenian Religion* (1996); sacrifice (the *thysia* and the *hecatomb*), the festival calendar (Panathenaia, Dionysia, Thesmophoria, Adonis), the *pax deorum*; pollution (*miasma*) and purification; the *trittys* and the calendar of the *polis*.
2. **Oracles, omens, and the management of uncertainty** — Delphi and the Pythia (the ethylene gas debate), Dodona, the *kleroi*; the *manteis*; curse tablets and binding magic (*defixiones*); the Greek Magical Papyri.
3. **Mystery and salvation** — Eleusis (the *myesis*, the *epopteia*, the Homeric Hymn to Demeter), the Orphic gold leaves, Dionysiac *thiasoi*, the *theoxenia*, the healing cult of Asclepius at Epidaurus.
4. **The philosophical critique** — Xenophanes ("Ethiopians say their gods are snub-nosed and black"), Heraclitus, Parmenides; Socrates' *daimonion* and the trial of 399 BCE ("not believing in the gods of the city"); Plato's *Phaedo*, the *Timaeus*, the *Republic*'s "noble lie"; Aristotle's *Metaphysics* Λ and the unmoved mover.
5. **Hellenistic philosophies as religions** — Epicurus (the gods as indifferent, *ataraxia*), the Stoics (the *logos*, *oikeiōsis*, Marcus Aurelius), the Skeptics; the emergence of the "sage" as soteriological figure.
6. **Concept in Focus:** *Eusebeia* (piety) and *deisidaimonia* (superstition) — how the Greeks drew the boundary we would call "religion vs. superstition."

---

### Chapter 14 — Warring States China: Heaven, Ritual, and the Way (pp. 263–280)

**Thesis.** The Chinese religious tradition was forged in a period of political collapse, and its answer to chaos was not salvation but the rectification of the human order.

1. **Background** — Shang oracle-bone divination (the *di* and the ancestors), the Zhou conquest and the *Mandate of Heaven* (*tianming*), the *Book of Songs*, the *Book of Documents*, the *Zhou Li*.
2. **The ritual revolution** — *li* (rite, propriety) as the technology of social harmony; the mourning system (*wufu*, the five degrees of mourning); the "ritualization of the everyday" (Roost-Kermani).
3. **Confucius (551–479 BCE)** — the *Analects*, *ren* (humaneness), *li*, the *junzi*, filial piety (*xiao*), the "rectification of names," the *dao* of the former kings; the Master as a failed bureaucrat and a successful teacher; the disciples.
4. **The Hundred Schools** — Mozi and *jian'ai* ("universal love"), his arguments against offensive war and against music/ritual expense; the "Mohist logic" tradition; the Legalists (Shang Yang, Han Feizi) and the state as ritual replacement; Yang Zhu's "egoism" (the Mencius critique of *yang zhu*).
5. **Mengzi (Mencius, c. 372–289 BCE) and Xunzi (c. 310–235 BCE)** — the "goodness of human nature" vs. the "nature is bad, ritual is the cure"; Xunzi's "Discourse on Heaven" — an early naturalist attack on omens.
6. **The *Daodejing* and Zhuangzi** — *dao*, *de*, *wu wei*, *ziran*, the "uncarved block"; the dating of the text (Warring States composite vs. a single Laozi); Zhuangzi's butterfly, the *qi*, the "fasting of the mind"; the problem of "Daoism" as philosophy vs. "Daoism" as religion (the "three teachings" problem).

**Text in Focus:** The Guodian bamboo slips (c. 300 BCE) — *Tai yi sheng shui* ("The Great One Generates Water") — an early cosmogony linking Daoist and Confucian themes.

# PART III — THE AGE OF EMPIRES AND THE BOOK RELIGIONS, c. 200 BCE – 600 CE (pp. 281–410)

*Framing problem: the age of Rome, Persia, the Han, and the Kushans produced the first "world religions" — traditions portable enough to cross empires, textual enough to survive exile, and institutionally flexible enough to outlive the states that sponsored them.*

---

### Chapter 15 — Hellenistic and Roman Religion: Empire, Cult, and the Marketplace of the Gods (pp. 285–302)

**Thesis.** Roman religion was a technology of civic peace; what destroyed it was not disbelief but the arrival of a new kind of cult demanding exclusive loyalty.

1. **Hellenistic transformations** — Alexander and the ruler cult; the diffusion of Isis/Serapis; the *interpretatio graeca*; the "oriental" cults and the ancient debate about "spiritualization."
2. **Roman religion proper** — *pax deorum*, the *pontifices* and the *augures*, the Vestals, the *Fasti*, the *lectisternium*, the triumph; the household *lararium* and the *Lares* and *Penates*.
3. **The imperial cult** — the *divus* of the emperors, the provincial *seviri*, the *Ara Pacis*; the "politics of the sacred" and the question of "belief" in the imperial cult (Ando).
4. **Persecution and crisis** — the Bacchanalian scandal (186 BCE), the expulsions of astrologers, the *senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus*; the crisis of the third century, Sol Invictus and Aurelian (274 CE).
5. **The "religious marketplace"** — Mithras and the *tauroctony* (the Mithraeum under Santa Prisca), Cybele and Attis, the *taurobolium*, the *votives*; conversions in the ancient world (the "God-fearers" and the synagogue's appeal).
6. **The Roman concept of the sacred** — *religio*, *superstitio*, *sacrum*; Augustine's later reuse of *religio* (as the inner posture of the heart) and its long-term theological consequences.

**Debate Box:** *Was Roman religion "in decline" before Christianity?* (MacMullen's *Christianizing the Roman Empire*, Salzman's *The Making of a Christian Aristocracy*.)

---

### Chapter 16 — From Jesus to Christ: The Making of Christianity (pp. 303–320)

**Thesis.** Christianity began as a movement within Second Temple Judaism; its distinctiveness emerged from a resurrection claim, a text-based missionary program, and an unusually aggressive institutional consolidation.

1. **The historical Jesus** — sources and criteria (independent attestation, dissimilarity, coherence, embarrassment); the Galilean ministry, the parables, the healings, the table fellowship with "sinners," the temple action, the *Logia*, the *Synoptics*, and the *Gospel of Thomas*.
2. **The passion and the resurrection claim** — the crucifixion under Pilate (c. 30 CE); Paul's *1 Corinthians 15* list; the "visionary" vs. "empty tomb" debates; the *Zealot* hypothesis and its decline (Horsley, Casey, Aslan's popular reading).
3. **Paul and the diaspora mission** — the letters (c. 50–60 CE), the "Paul and Judaism" re-evaluation (E. P. Sanders's "covenantal nomism," Stendahl, Boyarin's *The Jewish Gospel*); Galatians 3:28; the "parting of the ways" question (see Ch. 18).
4. **The lost Christianities** — the Ebionites, the Marcionites, the Montanists, the "Gospel of the Hebrews," the *Shepherd of Hermas*; Nag Hammadi (1945) and the *Gospel of Truth*, the *Apocryphon of John*, the *Thunder: Perfect Mind*.
5. **Canon and heresy** — Irenaeus's *Against Heresies* (c. 180), the *Muratorian Fragment*, Athanasius's 39th Festal Letter (367 CE) — the first list of the 27 New Testament books; the "canon as a sociological act."
6. **Persecution and martyrdom** — Pliny's letter to Trajan (c. 112 CE), the Scillitan martyrs (180), the Decian persecution (250), the *passiones* and the cult of the martyrs; the ambivalence of Christian attitudes to persecution (Tertullian's "the blood of the martyrs…").

---

### Chapter 17 — Constantine, Nicaea, and the Christianization of the Roman World (pp. 321–338)

**Thesis.** The conversion of the empire did not make Christianity more "spiritual"; it made it a governing institution, and the resulting theological disputes were simultaneously constitutional crises.

1. **Constantine (306–337)** — the *Vita Constantini*, the vision accounts (Lactantius vs. Eusebius), the Milvian Bridge (312), the Edict of Milan (313), the Lateran basilica, the Council of Arles (314), the Donatist schism, the founding of Constantinople (330).
2. **Nicaea (325) and the Arian controversy** — Arius of Alexandria, the *homoousios*, the *Thalia*, Eusebius of Nicomedia; the "Second Arian Controversy" and the Councils of Serdica (343), Constantinople (381).
3. **The Cappadocian synthesis** — Basil of Caesarea's *On the Holy Spirit*, Gregory of Nyssa's *Life of Moses*, Gregory of Nazianzus; the doctrine of the Trinity as an "apophatic" solution; the *Filioque* origins.
4. **The Christological century** — Nestorius and the Council of Ephesus (431), Chalcedon (451) and the *Tome of Leo*; the "Oriental Orthodox" split (Coptic, Armenian, Syriac, Ethiopian) and the mono-energist/mono-physite debates (the *Henotikon*, the Council of Constantinople 681).
5. **Theodosius and the end of the pagan state** — the Edict of Thessalonica (380), the anti-pagan legislation of 391–392, the destruction of the Serapeum (391), Ambrose vs. Theodosius over the Callinicum synagogue; the "conversion" debate (MacMullen vs. Markus).
6. **Augustine (354–430)** — the *Confessions*, Manichaean and Neoplatonist influences, the Pelagian controversy, *The City of God*, the doctrine of original sin and grace; the afterlife of Augustine in the Reformation (Luther's "monk's trick").

**Object in Focus:** The Chi-Rho monogram on the *Missorium of Theodosius* (388 CE) — the Christian symbol as imperial furniture.

---

### Chapter 18 — The Rabbis and the Making of Judaism (pp. 339–356)

**Thesis.** Rabbinic Judaism and Christianity are twin responses to the destruction of the Second Temple; the "parting of the ways" was neither sudden nor symmetrical.

1. **70 CE and after** — the fall of the Temple (Josephus), the *Yavneh* ("Jamnia") myth and its dismantling (S. Cohen, Lapin); the end of sacrifice and the *Korban* liturgy; the synagogue as new sacred space; the *Birkat ha-Minim* debate and the "blessing against the heretics."
2. **The rabbinic canon** — the *Mishnah* (c. 200 CE, Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi), the *Tosefta*, the *baraitot*, the *Jerusalem Talmud* (c. 400), the *Babylonian Talmud* (c. 500–600); the *midrash* (Genesis Rabbah, Leviticus Rabbah), the *Mekhilta*, the *Sifra*.
3. **Rabbinic methods** — the thirteen *middot* of Rabbi Ishmael, *kal va-chomer*, the *pilpul*; the concept of *Torah she-b'al peh* (the Oral Torah) and the self-legitimation of the rabbinic class.
4. **The "parting of the ways" debate** — the gradual model (Becker & Reed, *The Ways That Never Parted*), Daniel Boyarin's "border lines"; the "Jewish Christianity" that persisted (the Nazarenes, the Elkesaites).
5. **Material evidence** — the Dura-Europos synagogue (244 CE) and its frescoes; Beth Alpha and Sepphoris; the synagogue of Ostia; Jewish magical amulets and Babylonian incantation bowls.
6. **Practice and identity** — the *tefillah*, the *Shabbat*, the *kashrut*, the *mikveh*, the *metzitzah*, the *beit din*; the "four cubits of *halakha*" and the "lived religion" question.

---

### Chapter 19 — Gnosis and Its Rivals: Gnosticism, Hermeticism, Manichaeism (pp. 357–374)

**Thesis.** The "heretical" systems of late antiquity were not failures of orthodoxy but fully realized religions of knowledge; the category "Gnosticism" itself is a polemical artifact.

1. **The category problem** — Michael Williams's *Rethinking "Gnosticism"* (1996) and Karen King's *What Is Gnosticism?* — the Irenaean construction of "the Gnostic heresy" as a single system.
2. **The Nag Hammadi library** (discovered 1945) — the *Gospel of Truth*, the *Apocryphon of John*, the *Gospel of Thomas*, the *Thunder: Perfect Mind*, the *Gospel of Philip* (the "bridal chamber"); the Coptic manuscripts and the dating (c. 350–400 CE, copying older Greek originals).
3. **The myth of the divine spark** — Sophia's fall, the Demiurge (Yaldabaoth), the Archons, the *pleroma*, the *kenoma*, Valentinus and the "threefold" anthropology (pneumatic, psychic, hylic); the "Sethian" and "Valentinian" schools.
4. **Hermeticism** — the *Corpus Hermeticum*, the *Asclepius*, the *Poimandres*; "As above, so below"; the *Tabula Smaragdina*; the Hermetic "religion of the mind."
5. **Mani (216–277 CE) and Manichaeism** — the *Religion of Light* as the first missionary "world religion" (Syriac, Coptic, Middle Persian, Sogdian, Chinese *móníjiào*); the "living" and "hearing" elect; the cosmogony of the *Primal Man*; Augustine's nine years as a Manichaean "hearer" and his later polemic.
6. **The sociology of heterodoxy** — why "heresies" thrived in cities, why they attracted women (Elaine Pagels's *The Gnostic Gospels*), and how the church's polemic produced the modern image of the "Gnostic."

---

### Chapter 20 — Dharma Transformed: The Gupta Synthesis and the Classical Age of Hinduism (pp. 375–392)

**Thesis.** The period c. 200–600 CE produced the forms of "Hinduism" recognizable today: the temple, the image, the *Purāṇa*, the *bhakti* hymn, and the theology of divine grace.

1. **From Vedic ritual to *Purāṇic* myth** — the eighteen *Mahāpurāṇas*, the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa*, the *Viṣṇu* and *Mārkaṇḍeya* traditions; the *Bhagavad Gītā* (c. 200 BCE–200 CE) and its three paths (*jñāna*, *karma*, *bhakti*); the *Gītā*'s modern history (Gandhi's *Anāsaktiyoga*, Tilak, Aurobindo, Huxley, Oppenheimer).
2. **Temple Hinduism** — the *Āgamas* and the *Tantras*, the *pratiṣṭhā* (image consecration), *pūjā*, *darśana* (Diana Eck), *prasad*; the Gupta temple (Deogarh, Udayagiri, Bhitargaon, Aihole and the *Aihole Inscription* of Pulakesin II).
3. **Śaivism and Vaiṣṇavism** — the *Nāyaṉār* and *Āḻvār* saints (6th–9th c.), Nammāḻvār, Manikkavasagar; Śiva as *Mahādeva*; the *Pañcarātra*; the *Devī Māhātmya* and the Goddess traditions; *tantra* as a "religion of power" (Sanderson, White).
4. **Law and society** — the *Dharmaśāstras* (Manu, Yājñavalkya), the *varṇa* and *jati* system, the *āśrama* ideal, the *gotra*; the "caste as religion" question; the "Sanskritization" model (M. N. Srinivas) and its critics.
5. **The "Hinduism" question** — the term's colonial history (the *OED* dating, the "Hindu" as Persian geographical term), the *Sanātana Dharma* reformulation, the "one religion or many?" debate (Thapar, Flood, Malinar).
6. **Buddhism and Jainism in the same landscape** — the great monasteries (Nālandā, c. 5th c.), the *Vikramaśīla*; the beginnings of Buddhist tantra and the *Vajrayāna* turn.

---

### Chapter 21 — Paths of the Buddha after Aśoka: Theravāda, Mahāyāna, and the Silk Road (pp. 393–410)

**Thesis.** Buddhism's survival across Asia depended on the invention of the *bodhisattva* ideal and the *sūtra* as a portable "text-relic."

1. **Theravāda and the Pāli canon** — the *Tipiṭaka* (Vinaya, Sutta, Abhidhamma); the *Kathāvatthu*, the *Milindapañha* ("Questions of King Milinda"); the *Abhidhamma* as a "science of mind"; the *Visuddhimagga* (Buddhaghosa, 5th c.); the spread to Sri Lanka (the *Mahāvaṃsa*), the Mon, the Khmer (Angkor), the Pyu.
2. **The Mahāyāna turn** — the *Prajñāpāramitā* literature (the *Aṣṭasāhasrikā*, the *Heart Sūtra*, the *Diamond Sūtra* — the world's oldest dated printed book, 868 CE); Nāgārjuna (c. 150–250) and *śūnyatā*; the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*; the two truths doctrine.
3. **The *bodhisattva* ideal and celestial Buddhas** — the *Bodhisattvabhūmi*, the *bhūmis*, the "perfections" (*pāramitās*); Amitābha and Sukhāvatī (the *Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha*), Avalokiteśvara, Mañjuśrī; the *Saddharmapuṇḍarīka* (Lotus Sūtra) and *upāya* (skillful means); the *Vimalakīrti Nirdeśa*.
4. **Yogācāra and logic** — Asaṅga and Vasubandhu (4th–5th c.), the *Ālayavijñāna* (storehouse consciousness); Dignāga and Dharmakīrti and the "Buddhist logic" tradition; the *pramāṇa* debate with Nyāya.
5. **Central Asia and the Silk Road** — Gandhāra and the first anthropomorphic Buddha (c. 1st–2nd c. CE, the "Greek Buddha" debate); Dunhuang and the Mogao caves (the "Library Cave," sealed c. 1000, rediscovered 1900); Kumārajīva (344–413) and the translation enterprise; the first Chinese monks and nuns (Zhu Shixing, Fayao).
6. **The arrival of Buddhism in China** — the *míngfó* debate ("the spirit extinguishes"), the *Mouzi Lihuolun* (c. 200 CE), the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* translation; the beginnings of the Chinese Buddhist "schools."

---

# PART IV — THE GREAT UNIVERSAL FAITHS, 600–1200 (pp. 411–540)

*Framing problem: the era of Islam's rise, of the Buddhist and Confucian synthesis in East Asia, of Byzantine Christendom and the papal reform. Each of these traditions became "universal" in a distinct way — by conquest, by translation, by empire, or by pastoral care.*

---

### Chapter 22 — The Quran and the Rise of Islam (pp. 415–432)

**Thesis.** Islam began as a prophetic movement in a caravan sanctuary and became, within a century, the constitutional framework of a world empire.

1. **Late Antique Arabia** — the Himyarite kingdom and the Jewish kingdom of Dhū Nuwās (c. 523 CE); the Christian Najran; the Hanifs; the *Jāhiliyya* poetry and the *mu'allaqāt*; the Ka'ba as a *haram* sanctuary; the *Sana'a palimpsest* and the text-critical question.
2. **Muḥammad (c. 570–632)** — the *hira* on Mount Hira; the Meccan suras (the *Sūrat al-Muddaththir*, the *Mu'awwidhatayn*), the opposition of the Quraysh, the Hijra (622) and the *Constitution of Medina*, the Badr/ Uhud/ Khandaq campaigns, the Treaty of Ḥudaybiya (628), the conquest of Mecca (630), the Farewell Sermon.
3. **The Quran as text** — the *Meccan* vs. *Medinan* divisions; the *Qira'at* (the readings) and the Uthmanic codex; the *mu'atta* debate; the modern "Quranic studies" controversies (Crone & Cook's *Hagarism*, Shoemaker's *The Death of a Prophet*, Gabriel Said Sinai's *The Quran*); the *Asbāb al-nuzūl*.
4. **The first *fitna*** — the *Ridda* wars (632–633), the conquest of the Sasanian empire (Qādisiyyah 636–637, Nihavand 642), the *ṣaḥāba* and the caliphal question; the murder of Uthman (656) and the Battle of the Camel; Ṣiffīn (657), the Kharijites and the *murji'a*; the Umayyads and the *ma'ālim* (Mu'awiya 661).
5. **The Sunni/Shia split** — the *imamate* doctrine, the Twelve Imams, the *ghayba* (occultation), the *Karbala* narrative (680) and the *Ashura*; the *Zaydis*, the *Ismailis*, the *Fatimids*; the Dome of the Rock (691) and the "message of Islam" inscription.
6. **The early community's self-understanding** — the *hadith* literature and the *sīra* (Ibn Isḥāq); the *ummah* as a new kind of community; the *dhimmī* status and the *Pact of Umar*.

---

### Chapter 23 — The Expansion of Islam and the Making of Islamic Civilization (pp. 433–450)

**Thesis.** The "Golden Age" of Islam was a translation culture: the deliberate recovery of Greek, Persian, and Indian knowledge in the service of a new intellectual and legal order.

1. **Conquest and conversion** — al-Andalus (711), Sind (711–713), the Maghreb, the *jizya*, the *dhimmī* system's legal logic; Richard Bulliet's "conversion curve" (c. 800–1000 CE as the pivotal century in Egypt, Iran, and Central Asia); the *mawālī* problem and the *shu'ubiyya*.
2. **The Abbasid revolution (750)** — the *da'wa*, the Hashimiyya, the Battle of the Zab (750); Baghdad (762) and the *Bayt al-Ḥikma*; the *Translation Movement* (Hunayn ibn Isḥāq, al-Kindi, al-Farabi); the *mu'tazila* and the *mihna* (833–848); al-Ash'ari (d. 936) and the "Ash'ari synthesis."
3. **Law and hadith** — the *six books* (Bukhari, Muslim, Abu Dawud, Tirmidhi, Nasai, Ibn Majah), the *isnad* and the *matn*; the four Sunni *madhhabs* (Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, Hanbali); the Ja'fari school; the *ulama* and the "closure of the gate of *ijtihad*" thesis (Melchert's counter-argument).
4. **Shia intellectual traditions** — the *Ithna Ashari* imamate, the *Safavid* turn (1501), the Ismaili *da'i* network (Nasir-i Khusraw), the *Assassins* myth (Marco Polo vs. Marshall Hodgson); the *Aga Khan* and the Nizari.
5. **Science, philosophy, and mysticism** — the *Encyclopedia of the Brethren of Purity* (*Ikhwan al-Safa*); Ibn Sina (Avicenna) and the *Proof of the Truthful*; al-Biruni's *India* (c. 1030); al-Ghazali (1058–1111) and the *Incoherence of the Philosophers*, the *Revival of the Religious Sciences*; Ibn Rushd (Averroes) and the Latin reception.
6. **Sufism and the popular orders** — the *dhikr*, the *khanaqah*, the *salasil*; al-Junayd, Rabia al-Adawiyya, the *Qalandars*; the *tariqa* as social institution (the Qadiriyya, the Naqshbandiyya, the Chishtiyya).

---

### Chapter 24 — The Tao and the Ancestors: Daoism, Buddhism, and the State in China (pp. 451–468)

**Thesis.** China's religious history is a story of institutional competition and mutual borrowing: the "three teachings" never merged, but they learned to coexist under a Confucian state.

1. **The institutionalization of Daoism** — the *Tianshi dao* (Celestial Masters, 142 CE), the *Taiping jing*, the "Way of Great Peace," the *Ordination Registers* and the *lu*; the Lingbao and Shangqing revelations (4th c.); the *Daozang* canon (compiled 1445).
2. **Buddhism in China: the Six Dynasties** — the *sangha* and the state (the "ordination certificates"), the *dharma-ending age* (*mofa*), the *Prajñā* schools, the *Sanlun* (Kumārajīva), the *Tiantai* (Zhiyi 538–597), the *Huayan* (Fazang 643–712), the *Pure Land* (Huiyuan, Tanluan, Shandao), the *Chan* origin legend (Bodhidharma), the *Platform Sutra* (Shenneng 638–713).
3. **Tang cosmopolitanism and its reversal** — Xuanzang (602–664) and the *Great Tang Records on the Western Regions*; the *Huichang* persecution (845); the *Jinming jing* and the *Amitabha* devotional movement; the *Maitreya* rebellions (the *White Lotus* genealogy).
4. **Song religious ferment** — the *Chan* "public cases" (*gong'an*) and the *Linji* and *Caodong* lineages; "gentry Buddhism" (the *Gergory* and *Zeuschner* debate); the *Neo-Confucian* synthesis: Zhou Dunyi, the Cheng brothers, Zhu Xi (1130–1200) and the *Four Books*, the *li/qi* metaphysics, the *investigation of things*; Wang Yangming (1472–1529) and the *innate knowing*.
5. **The "three teachings" and popular religion** — *sanjiao heyi* (the three teachings in harmony), the *Trinity* imagery (*Confucius, Laozi, Buddha*), the *Sanyuan* festival, the *chenghuang* (city god) cult, the *wenshang* (literati) deity cult, the *spirit-writing* (*fuji*) societies.
6. **Ritual and the family** — the *Jiali* (Zhu Xi's family rites), ancestor tablets, the *qingming* festival, the *yin-yang* masters, the *fengshui* debate; the "Chinese religion" question (C. K. Yang's *Religion in Chinese Society*).

---

### Chapter 25 — The Lands of the Rising Sun and the Mountain Kingdom: Japan and Tibet (pp. 469–486)

**Thesis.** In Japan and Tibet, Buddhism did not displace the indigenous cults but absorbed them through a set of sophisticated "identity" doctrines (*honji suijaku*, *terma*, *tulku*) — a case study in religious invention.

1. **Japan: the arrival** — the *Nihon Shoki* account (552/538 CE), the Soga clan, the *Shitennō-ji*, the *Asuka* temples; the *Nara* period (710–794), the *Kegon* and *Hossō* schools, the *Tōdai-ji* and the *Daibutsu*, the *Shōsōin* treasury.
2. **Shinto's "invention"** — the problem of *shinto* as a category (Grapard, Teeuwen, Isomae); the *Kojiki* (712) and *Nihon Shoki* (720); the *kami* cult, the *jinja*, the *norito* liturgies; the *honji suijaku* ("original ground and manifest trace") doctrine and the *Ryōbu Shintō* synthesis.
3. **Heian Buddhism** — Saichō (Dengyō, 767–822) and the *Tendai* platform at Enryaku-ji; Kūkai (774–835) and *Shingon* (*sokushin jōbutsu* — "attaining Buddhahood in this very body"), the *mandala*, the *Womb and Diamond* realms; Genshin (942–1017) and the *Ōjōyōshū* (the *nembutsu*).
4. **Kamakura "new Buddhism"** — Hōnen (1133–1212) and *senju nembutsu*; Shinran (1173–1263) and *tariki* ("other power"), the *Kyōgyōshinshō*, the "neither monk nor layman" (*sōzoku*); Dōgen (1200–1253) and the *Shōbōgenzō*, the *shikantaza* ("just sitting"); Nichiren (1222–1282) and the *Lotus Sūtra*, the *Namu-myōhō-renge-kyō*, the "risshō ankokuron"; the *Nichiren* movements in the modern era (Sōka Gakkai).
5. **Tibet: Bon and the imperial period** — the *Bon* tradition and its contested antiquity; Songtsen Gampo (617–649) and the *first diffusion* (*snga dar*), the *Tibetan alphabet*, the *Samye* monastery; Lang Darma's persecution (842); the *second diffusion* (*phyi dar*) and Atisha (1042); the *Sarma* (Kagyu, Sakya, Kadam) and *Nyingma* schools; the *terma* ("treasure") tradition.
6. **The Gelug and the "Dalai Lama" institution** — Tsongkhapa (1357–1419) and the *Lamrim*; the *Ganden* monastery; the "Great Fifth" (1617–1682) and the *Mongol* alliance; the *Panchen Lama* dispute; the *Tibetan Book of the Dead* (*Bardo Thodol*) and its Western reception (Evans-Wentz 1927).

**Object in Focus:** The *Sand Mandala* and its deliberate destruction — impermanence as a ritual technology.

---

### Chapter 26 — Christendom Divided: Byzantium, Rome, and the Christian East (pp. 487–504)

**Thesis.** Medieval Christendom was never one thing: the Byzantine, Latin, Coptic, Armenian, and Syriac churches developed distinct liturgies, theologies, and political theologies.

1. **Byzantine theology and piety** — the *iconoclast* controversy (726–843), John of Damascus's *On the Divine Images*, the *Triumph of Orthodoxy* (843); the *Divine Liturgy* (the *Chrysostom* and *Basil* rites), the *hesychast* controversy (Gregory Palamas, the *Tabor Light*); the *Hesychia* movement and the *Philokalia*.
2. **The "Great Schism" (1054)** — the *filioque*, papal primacy, *Azymes* (unleavened bread), the *Photian schism* (863–867); Runciman's "it was not a schism but a slow divorce" thesis (Dvornik, Meyendorff).
3. **The Latin West** — Benedict of Nursia (c. 480–547) and the *Rule*; the *Benedictine* expansion (Cluny 910, Cîteaux 1098, Bernard of Clairvaux); the papacy: Gregory I (590–604), the *Donation of Constantine* forgery, the *Investiture Contest* (1075–1122) and Gregory VII's *Dictatus Papae*; the *Canon Law* revolution (Gratian's *Decretum*, c. 1140).
4. **The schools and the friars** — Anselm's *Cur Deus Homo*, Abelard and Heloise, the *Chartres* cathedral school, the *Summa* tradition (Aquinas 1225–1274, *Summa Theologiae*, the "five ways"); Francis of Assisi (1181/82–1226) and the *Canticle of the Sun*, the *Cistercian* and *Franciscan* poverty debates; the *Dominican* preaching, the *Inquisition*'s legal invention (1231).
5. **The Christian East beyond Byzantium** — the *Coptic* church (the *Desert Fathers*, the *Monastery of St. Anthony*), the *Armenian* church (the *Etchmiadzin* and the "Gregory the Illuminator" tradition), the *Ethiopian Orthodox* tradition (the *Kebra Nagast*, the Ark narrative, the Solomonic dynasty); the *Church of the East* and its Silk Road expansion (the *Xi'an stele*, 781 CE; the "Nestorian" label and its problems).
6. **Martyrdom and inter-religious contact** — the *Reconquista* and the *Convivencia* in Iberia; the *Crusades* as a Christian institution (see Ch. 29).

**Key Concept:** *Caesaropapism* vs. *the two swords* — two Christian theories of political authority.

---

### Chapter 27 — Piety, Polemic, and Coexistence: Jews and Christians under Islam (pp. 505–522)

**Thesis.** The "Golden Age" of Jewish life in the Islamic world was real but fragile; its achievements were made possible by a legal order that was simultaneously protective and hierarchical.

1. **The "Golden Age" thesis** — the *Convivencia* debate in al-Andalus (Américo Castro vs. Claudio Sánchez-Albornoz; Jane Gerber, Mark Cohen's *Under Crescent and Cross*); the *dhimmi* framework and the *Pact of Umar*.
2. **The Cairo Geniza** — the *Ben Ezra* synagogue (Fustat), Solomon Schechter's 1896 removal, the *Taylor-Schechter* collection; the letters, contracts, *responsa*, the *ketubbot*; the *Karaites* and the *Rabbanite* dispute; the "documentary" life of medieval Jews.
3. **The intellectual exchange** — Saadia Gaon (882–942) and the *Kitāb al-Amānāt*; the *Kalam* and Jewish philosophy; Bahya ibn Paquda's *Duties of the Heart*; Judah Halevi's *Kuzari* (the "argument against the philosophers"); Maimonides (1138–1204) — the *Mishneh Torah*, the *Guide for the Perplexed*, the "Maimonidean controversy" (the *Maimonidean* ban of 1232).
4. **Polemic and its literary forms** — the *Muqabala* (dialogue) genre; the *Apology of al-Kindi* (a Christian polemic against Islam); the *Risāla* of al-Samaw'al; the "dhimmi" status in practice (the *ahl al-dhimma* and the *hudud* restrictions).
5. **Persecution and its variants** — the 1033 Fez massacre, the *Almohad* persecutions (1121–1269) and the forced conversions of Maimonides' family; the *Mawza Exile* (1679) in Yemen; the *Yellow badge* debate in the Islamic world.
6. **The eastern spread of Islam** — the *Delhi Sultanate*, the *Sufi* orders in India (the *Chishtiyya*, Moinuddin Chishti, Nizamuddin Auliya); the *Malacca* sultanate and the Islamization of maritime Southeast Asia (the *Wali Sanga* in Java, Sunan Kalijaga); the *Swahili* coast (Kilwa, the *Mosque of Kizimkazi*).

---

### Chapter 28 — The Living Sacred: Religion and Everyday Life in the High Middle Ages (pp. 523–540)

**Thesis.** "Popular religion" is not a degraded version of elite religion but a distinct system — and the medieval world's most important religious developments happened at the parish, the shrine, and the plague hospital.

1. **The parish and the liturgical year** — the *Mass* and the *Eucharistic* controversy (Lanfranc vs. Berengar; the Fourth Lateran Council 1215 and *transubstantiation*); the *cult of the saints*, the *relics* economy (the *translation* of relics, the *Holy Blood* controversies), the *Marian* cult (Notre-Dame, the *Immaculate Conception* debate).
2. **Pilgrimage and the holy landscape** — Santiago de Compostela (the *Codex Calixtinus*), Canterbury (the murder of Becket 1170), Jerusalem, Rome; the *Stations of the Cross*; the *Kumbh Mela* and the Indian pilgrimage complex (the *tirtha*); the *Hajj* and its infrastructure (the *Mamluk* administration of the *Haramayn*).
3. **Devotional movements and dissent** — the *Beguines* (Hadewijch, Mechtild of Magdeburg, Marguerite Porete — burned 1310), the *Brethren of the Common Life* (the *Devotio Moderna*, the *Imitation of Christ*); the *Cathars* and the *Albigensian Crusade* (1209–29), the *Waldensians* (Peter Waldo), the *Lollards* (Wycliffe).
4. **The Black Death (1347–51) and its religious consequences** — the *flagellants*, the *pogroms* (Strasbourg 1349, the *Black Death persecutions*), the *Dance of Death*, the *Avignon Papacy* (1309–77) and the *Western Schism* (1378–1417), the *Conciliarist* movement (Constance 1414–18, the burning of Hus 1415).
5. **Cross-cultural mysticism: a controlled comparison** — Meister Eckhart (c. 1260–1328) and *Gelassenheit*; Bernard of Clairvaux's *Sermons on the Song of Songs*; al-Ghazali's *Ihya'*, Ibn Arabi (1165–1240) and *wahdat al-wujud*; Rumi (1207–73) and the *Masnavi*, the *Whirling Dervishes*; the *Bhakti* mystics (see Ch. 32). *Comparative questions:* What is the same? What is different? What is gained and lost by comparison?
6. **Medieval anti-Judaism** — the *blood libel* (Norwich 1144), the *host desecration* accusations, the *Fourth Lateran Council*'s badge requirement, the *expulsions* (England 1290, France 1306/1394, Spain 1492).

**Text in Focus:** Marguerite Porete's *The Mirror of Simple Souls* (c. 1300) — a laywoman's mystical treatise that was condemned, burned, and then forgotten for six centuries.

# PART V — REFORMATIONS AND GLOBAL ENTANGLEMENTS, c. 1250–1700 (pp. 541–670)

*Framing problem: the early modern world was not "pre-modern." It was the era of print, gunpowder empires, and the first truly global circulation of religions — and of the witch trials, which showed how much sacred power remained in the ordinary world.*

---

### Chapter 29 — The Mongol Moment and the Religions of the Steppe (pp. 545–562)

**Thesis.** The Mongol empire was the first genuinely global religious marketplace, and the *Pax Mongolica* reshaped every tradition it touched.

1. **The steppe religious repertoire** — Tengrism, the *Eternal Blue Sky*, the *ovoo*, shamanic *bö*, ancestor cults; the Khitan *Liao* and the *Jurchen*; the *Keraite* Christians and the *Nestorian* presence in the steppe.
2. **Genghis Khan (c. 1162–1227) and the *Yasa*** — the "law of the Eternal Sky," the *religious tolerance* policy; the "shamanic" versus "charismatic" reading of Mongol rule; the destruction of the *Khorasan* and the *Qara-Khitai*.
3. **Mongol religion in practice** — the *Ilkhanate* and the Buddhist conversion of Ghazan (1295); the *Yuan* dynasty and Tibetan Buddhism (Phagpa, the *Sakya* "priest-patron" relationship); the *Chagatai* and the *Timurid* Islamic synthesis.
4. **The Franciscan missions** — John of Plano Carpini (1245), William of Rubruck (1253–55), the *Karakorum* debates (1254), the *disputation* of the Christians, Muslims, and Buddhists before Möngke Khan — a medieval "interfaith dialogue."
5. **Marco Polo and the *Livre des Merveilles*** — the Mongol *yam* postal system, the *Silk Road* of the 13th c., the *Rabban Bar Sauma* (the "Reverse Marco Polo"), the *Ilkhanate* correspondence with Europe.
6. **Legacy** — the *Timurid* Renaissance (Ulugh Beg, the *Gur-e-Amir*), the *Ming* reaction, the *Shaybanid* and *Kazakh* khanates; the *Mongol* contribution to the global spread of Islam, Buddhism, and Christianity.

---

### Chapter 30 — Print, Prophecy, and the Protestant Reformation (pp. 563–580)

**Thesis.** The Reformation was a media revolution before it was a theological one: the printing press turned a monastic dispute into a mass movement.

1. **Late medieval reform** — the *Devotio Moderna*, the *conciliarist* movement, the *Humanists* (Erasmus's *Novum Instrumentum* 1516, the *Praise of Folly*), the *Lollards*, the *Hussites* (Jan Hus, the *Utraquists*, the *Taborite* radicalism).
2. **Luther (1483–1546)** — the *Ninety-Five Theses* (1517), the Leipzig Debate (1519), the *Diet of Worms* (1521), the *Turmerlebnis* and the *sola fide*/*sola scriptura*/*priesthood of all believers*; the *Bondage of the Will*; the *Peasants' War* (1524–25) and Luther's *Against the Murderous, Thieving Hordes*.
3. **The spread of print** — the *pamphlet* explosion (the *Flugschriften*), the *Luther Bible* (1522/1534), the *woodcuts* of Cranach, the *Reformation* as an image war; the *vernacular* Bible and its unintended consequences.
4. **The Reformed tradition** — Zwingli (1519 Zurich), the *Eucharistic* debate at Marburg (1529), Calvin (1509–1564) and the *Institutes* (1536), the *Geneva* model, the *consistory*, predestination; the *Anabaptists* (Münster 1534–35, the *Schleitheim Confession* 1527), the *Radical Reformation* (Williams's *Radical Reformation*).
5. **The English and Scottish reformations** — Henry VIII (1534), the *Dissolution of the Monasteries*, the *Book of Common Prayer* (1549/1552), the *Elizabethan Settlement* (1559), John Knox and the *Presbyterian* model; the *Puritan* movement and the *Pilgrims*.
6. **The social consequences** — the *Confessionalization* thesis (Reinhard, Schilling), the *witch craze*'s relationship to Reformation (see Ch. 33), the *Protestant* work ethic debate (Weber 1905 and the *Tawney*/*Samuelsson*/*Gorski* critiques).

---

### Chapter 31 — Catholic Renewal, the Jesuits, and the Global Mission (pp. 581–598)

**Thesis.** The Catholic Church answered the Reformation with a global institutional renewal — and in the process became the first truly transcontinental religious corporation.

1. **The Council of Trent (1545–1563)** — the *decrees* on justification, scripture, and tradition, the *Catechism* (1566), the *Index of Forbidden Books* (1559), the *Seminaries*, the *Breviary* and *Missal* reform (Pius V, 1570).
2. **The Society of Jesus** — Ignatius of Loyola (1491–1556) and the *Spiritual Exercises*, the *Formula of the Institute* (1540), the *Ratio Studiorum* (1599); the *Jesuit* "accommodation" missions: Matteo Ricci (1552–1610) in China (the *Chinese Rites Controversy*), Roberto de Nobili (1577–1656) in India (the *Malabar Rites*), the *Reductions* of Paraguay.
3. **The *Chinese Rites Controversy*** — Ricci's *Tianzhu Shiyi*, the *Dominican* and *Franciscan* objections, the *Congregation de Propaganda Fide* (1622), Clement XI's *Cum Deus Optimus* (1704), Kangxi's *Edict of Toleration* (1692) and the *Yongzheng* prohibition (1724) — a case study in the failure of cultural translation.
4. **Other Catholic orders** — the *Franciscans* in the Americas, the *Dominicans* in the Philippines and Mexico, the *Ursulines* (Angela Merici, the *education of women*), the *Vincentians* (Vincent de Paul, the *Charity*), the *Carmelite* reform (Teresa of Ávila, John of the Cross, the *Dark Night of the Soul*).
5. **The *Baroque* Catholic world** — the *sacred architecture* (Borromini, Bernini, the *Gesù*), the *miracle* cults, the *Immaculate Conception* and the *Sacred Heart*, the *witch-hunting* Catholic contexts (Spain, Italy).
6. **The mission and the colonial state** — the *Padroado* and the *Patronato*, the *Goa Inquisition* (1561–1812), the *Mission* and *Encomienda* system, the *Bartolomé de las Casas* debate (the *Valladolid* disputation 1550–51).

---

### Chapter 32 — Early Modern Islam: Ottomans, Safavids, Mughals, and the Sufi-Bhakti Synthesis (pp. 599–616)

**Thesis.** The "gunpowder empires" of the Islamic world institutionalized Sufism and produced a devotional synthesis that shaped the modern world.

1. **The Ottoman order** — the *Millet* system, the *Şeyhülislam*, the *Kanuni* (Suleiman, 1520–66), the *Janissaries* and the *devshirme*; the *Sufi* orders (the *Mevlevi*, the *Bektashi*, the *Naqshbandi*); the *coffeehouse* and the *mawlid* controversy (Ibn Taymiyyah's 14th c. fatwa and the Ottoman *mawlid* defense).
2. **The Safavid turn (1501)** — Ismail I and the *Qizilbash*, the *Twelver Shi'ism* imposed by state decree; the *Imamzadeh* shrines, the *Ashura* rituals (the *ta'ziyeh* passion plays), the *Usuli* vs. *Akhbari* debate, the *Marja'* system.
3. **The Mughal synthesis** — Babur (1526), Akbar (1556–1605) and the *Din-i Ilahi*, the *Ibadat Khana* debates (1575–78), the *Sulh-i Kul* ("universal peace"), the *Jizya* abolition (1564); Aurangzeb (1658–1707) and the *re-imposition*, the *Khutba* controversy, the *Sikh* response.
4. **The Bhakti movement** — the *Alvars* and *Nayanars*' legacy; Ramanuja (1017–1137) and *Vishishtadvaita*; Madhva (1238–1317); Kabir (1440–1518) and the *Bijak*; Mirabai (1498–1547); Tulsidas (1532–1623) and the *Ramcharitmanas*; Chaitanya (1486–1534) and the *Gaudiya* tradition; the *Lingayats* (Basavanna) and the *Virashaivas*; the *Sant* tradition and its *anti-caste* rhetoric.
5. **The Sikh tradition** — Guru Nanak (1469–1539), the *Gurdwara*, the *Langar*; the ten Gurus, the *Adi Granth* (1604), the *Khalsa* (1699) and Guru Gobind Singh; the *Singh Sabha* reform (1873) and the modern *Sikh* identity.
6. **Comparative question: what is "devotional religion"?** — the *bhakti* and *tariqa* as parallel solutions to the problem of ritual mediation and caste/social hierarchy.

---

### Chapter 33 — Witchcraft, Magic, and the Disenchantment of the World (pp. 617–634)

**Thesis.** The early modern witch craze was not a "survival" of paganism but a product of Christian demonology, printing, state formation, and social stress; and its decline tells us as much about the rise of modernity as its existence.

1. **The legal and theological construction of witchcraft** — the *Malleus Maleficarum* (1487), the *Formicarium* of Nider, the *bull* of Innocent VIII (*Summis Desiderantes*, 1484), the *witch's sabbath*, the *pact with the Devil*, the *maleficium*.
2. **The geography of the trials** — Germany, the *Basque* and *Lorraine* trials, the *East Anglia* outbreak (1645–47), the *Salem* trials (1692), the *Valais* and *Finnish* patterns; the *absence* of trials in Spain, Italy, and Russia (with notable exceptions) — and why.
3. **Who was accused** — the *gender* question (c. 75–80% women), the *social profile* (the *beggar*, the *midwife*, the *neighbor*), the *confession* and the *torture*; the "reversal of the social order" thesis (Erik Midelfort, Lyndal Roper).
4. **The "disenchantment" thesis** — Weber's *Entzauberung*; the "Rise of Magic" (Keith Thomas, *Religion and the Decline of Magic*, 1971) and the *complementary* argument; the *Stuart Clark* reading (*Thinking with Demons*, 1997) — witchcraft as a *coherent intellectual system*.
5. **Magic in the global context** — the *cunning folk* and the *white witch*; *Vodun* and the *Caribbean* (see Ch. 37); the *kangxi* "witchcraft" trials (1768, Philip Kuhn's *Soulstealers*); the *Navajo* *skinwalker*; the *global* problem of the "witch" as a category.
6. **The end of the trials** — the *Enlightenment* critique (the *Balthasar Bekker* debate), the *legal* reform (the *Trials* of the *Swedish* and *English* courts), the *18th c.* decline; the modern "witchcraft" revival (see Ch. 48).

---

### Chapter 34 — Late Imperial China and Tokugawa Japan: Religion and the State (pp. 635–652)

**Thesis.** In East Asia's late empires, the state actively managed religion, and the resulting "orthodoxies" were as much political as spiritual.

1. **Ming (1368–1644) and Qing (1644–1912) China** — the *Hu-Wang* controversy over *Neo-Confucian orthodoxy*; the *Wang Yangming* school; the *White Lotus* and *Millenarian* rebellions (the *Red Turban*, the *Eight Trigrams* rebellion of 1813); the *Yongzheng* emperor's *Great Collection of Writings on the Doctrine of the Mean* (1725) — the state's own "three teachings."
2. **Daoist and popular religion under the Qing** — the *Quanzhen* (Complete Perfection) order, the *Longmen* lineage; the *Daozang* reprint; the *fengshui* and *ancestor* practices as social institutions; the *shen* cults and the *chenghuang* (city god) as state-licensed local cults.
3. **The Chinese encounter with Christianity** — the *Jesuit* missions and the *Chinese Rites* prohibition (1724), the *White Lotus* *inculturated* Christianity, the *Taiping Rebellion* (1850–64) and Hong Xiuquan's "Heavenly Kingdom of Great Peace" — the bloodiest civil war of the 19th c. and its *millenarian* roots.
4. **Tokugawa Japan (1603–1868)** — the *kirishitan* (Christian) persecutions (the *Shimabara* rebellion 1637–38, the *sakoku* policy); the *terauke* (temple registration) system and the *Buddhist* temple as a *bureaucratic* organ; the *Kokugaku* (National Learning) movement (Motoori Norinaga, the *Kojiki Den*); the *Rangaku* (Dutch learning) and the *medical* and *astronomical* critiques.
5. **The "native studies" and the birth of modern Shinto** — the *Yoshida Shinto*, the *Ise* shrines, the *Kokka Shinto*; the *Meiji* *shrine* system (see Ch. 42).
6. **Korea and Vietnam** — the *Joseon* Neo-Confucian state and the *Buddhist* suppression; the *Silhak* (Practical Learning) movement; the *Cao Dai* and *Hoa Hao* (1920s–30s) — see Ch. 40.

---

### Chapter 35 — Religion and the New Science: Doubt, Deism, and the Roots of Secularity (pp. 653–670)

**Thesis.** The "scientific revolution" did not kill religion; it created a new category of the *natural* — and with it, a new concept of the *religious* as a separate sphere.

1. **The Copernican and Newtonian revolutions** — the *De Revolutionibus* (1543), the *Galileo* affair (1633) and the *Two Chief World Systems*; the *Newtonian* synthesis and the *General Scholium* ("This most beautiful system of the sun, planets, and comets, could only proceed from the counsel and dominion of an intelligent and powerful Being").
2. **The mechanical philosophy and the *secularization* of nature** — Descartes' *dualism*, the *Deus ex machina*, the *occasionalism* debate; the *corpuscular* philosophy; the "laws of nature" as a theological concept.
3. **The rise of *natural theology*** — the *Boyle Lectures*, the *design argument*, the *Watchmaker analogy*; the *Spectator* and the *Deists* (Matthew Tindal, *Christianity as Old as the Creation*, 1730); the *Latitudinarians*.
4. **The *radical* Enlightenment** — Spinoza's *Theological-Political Treatise* (1670) and *Tractatus*; the *Freethinkers* (John Toland, Anthony Collins); the *materialists* (La Mettrie's *Man a Machine*, Baron d'Holbach's *System of Nature*); the *Encyclopédie* (1751–72).
5. **The *Enlightenment* critique of religion** — Voltaire's *Dictionnaire Philosophique*, Hume's *Natural History of Religion* (1757) and *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (1779), Kant's *Religion Within the Limits of Reason Alone* (1793).
6. **The concept of "secularity"** — the *Taylorian* argument in *A Secular Age* (2007); the *Assad* critique of the "secularization" thesis in *Formations of the Secular* (2003); the "secular" as a *Christian* invention (the *saeculum*); the *Compagnon* problem of "how to think about the sacred after the sacred."

---

# PART VI — RELIGION IN THE AGE OF EMPIRE AND COLONIALISM, c. 1500–1900 (pp. 671–764)

*Framing problem: the same imperial expansion that destroyed indigenous worlds also created the global religions we now call "world religions." Colonialism is the crucible of modern religious identity.*

---

### Chapter 36 — The Conquest of the Americas and the Remaking of Indigenous Religions (pp. 675–692)

**Thesis.** The 1492 encounter produced a religious catastrophe, a forced conversion, and a new set of hybrid traditions that the colonial state could never fully control.

1. **Before 1492** — the *Aztec* (Mexica) world: *Huitzilopochtli*, the *tonalpohualli*, the *Templo Mayor*, the *sacrifice* debate (the *Tlaxcalan* and *Spanish* accounts, the *Mexica* self-representation in the *Florentine Codex*); the *Maya* (the *Popol Vuh*, the *k'uhul ajaw*), the *Inca* (*Inti*, the *Huaca*, the *Capacocha*, the *ceque* system).
2. **The conquest and the *extirpation of idolatry*** — the *Deus ex machina* of the *Requerimiento*; the *Franciscan* and *Dominican* missions; the *Guadalupan* apparition (1531) and the *Virgin of Guadalupe* as the *indigenizing* of Catholicism; the *Yucatán* *Book of Chilam Balam*.
3. **The *extirpators*** — José de Arriaga's *Extirpation of Idolatry in Peru* (1621), the *archival* *huaca* *reports*, the *quipu* and its *loss*; the *Pachamama* cult and the *Andean* *syncretism*.
4. **The *Pueblo* revolt (1680) and the *Maya* rebellions** — the *Popé* and the *re-conquest* of the *New Mexican* *pueblo*; the *Cruzob* and the *Talking Cross* (1850).
5. **The *African* and *mixed* populations** — the *Afro-Mexican* *cult* of *San Martín de Porres*, the *Santa Muerte* and the *Dia de los Muertos* — the *Catholic* *liturgical* *calendar* as a *survival* of *indigenous* and *African* forms.
6. **The *historiographical* debate** — the *demographic* *catastrophe* (the *Black Legend* vs. the *White Legend*), the *"spiritual conquest"* thesis (Ricardo Cividanes? — the *Ricardo* *Casanova* *thesis*), the *Jorge* *Klor de Alva* *argument* on *conversion* as *negotiation*.

---

### Chapter 37 — The African Diasporas and the Birth of Atlantic Religions (pp. 693–710)

**Thesis.** The religions of the African diaspora are the most creative religious inventions of the modern era — made under the most brutal conditions.

1. **West and Central African religious systems** — the *Yoruba* *Orisha* (Ogun, Oshun, Shango, Yemoja), the *Ifa* divination and the *Odu*; the *Kongo* *nkisi* and the *Simbi*; the *Akan* *abosom* and *akomfo*; the *Dahomey* *Vodun*.
2. **The *Middle Passage* and *slave* religion** — the *slave* *ship* as *ritual* *space*, the *maroon* *communities* (Palmares, the *Jamaican* *Maroons*), the *slave* *revolts* and *prophetic* *leadership* (the *Bacon's* *Rebellion*? — rather *Tacky's* *Revolt* 1760, the *Bussa* *Rebellion* 1816, the *Demerara* 1823).
3. **Haitian *Vodou*** — the *Bois* *Caïman* ceremony (August 1791), the *loa* and the *rada/petwo* *rites*, the *houngan* and *mambo*, the *Vodou* *of* *the* *Haitian* *Revolution* (1791–1804) — the *only* *successful* *slave* *revolt* and its *religious* *dimensions*.
4. **Brazilian *Candomblé* and *Umbanda*** — the *nagô*, *jeje*, and *angola* *nations*, the *casa* *grande* and the *senzala*; the *Macumba* and the *syncretic* *saints*; the *Umbanda* *codification* (1908); the *Quimbanda* and the *Exu*.
5. **The Caribbean** — *Santería* (Regla de Ocha) in *Cuba*, the *Lucumí* *tradition*, the *babalawo* and the *Ifá*; *Obeah* and *Myal* in *Jamaica*; the *Rastafari* movement (1930s) — the *Ethiopian* *apocalypse*, *Haile Selassie I*, the *Babylon* *of* *the* *West*.
6. **The *slave* *religion* and *Protestant* *evangelicalism*** — the *Great* *Awakening*, the *Baptist* and *Methodist* *mission* to *the* *slaves*, the *Black* *church* (the *AME* 1816, the *National* *Baptist* *Convention* 1895), the *spirituals* and the *theology* *of* *Exodus*.

---

### Chapter 38 — South Asia under Colonial Rule: Reform, Revival, and the Invention of "Hinduism" (pp. 711–728)

**Thesis.** Colonial knowledge and indigenous reform together produced the modern category "Hinduism" — a "religion" that was, in effect, a response to a colonial question.

1. **The *East India Company* and the *religion* *of* *the* *colonized*** — the *Somnath* *debate* (1843), the *Sati* *abolition* (1829), the *Bengal* *Renaissance*, the *Serampore* *Mission* (William Carey, 1793), the *Baptist* *mission* and the *Orientalist* *vs.* *Anglicist* *debate* (the *Minute* of 1835).
2. **Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833) and the *Brahmo* *Samaj*** — the *Tuhfat* *al*-*Muwahhidin*, the *Brahmo* *Samaj* (1828), the *Sati* *petition*, the *monotheistic* *Hinduism* *thesis*; the *Debendranath* *Tagore* and *Keshab* *Chandra* *Sen* *succession*.
3. **Dayananda Saraswati (1824–1883) and the *Arya* *Samaj*** — the *Satyarth* *Prakash* (1875), the *Vedic* *infallibility* *thesis*, the *Shuddhi* *reconversion* *movement*, the *cow* *protection* *movement* (1870s–1890s) and the *communal* *violence*.
4. **Ramakrishna (1836–1886) and Vivekananda (1863–1902)** — the *Kali* *devotion* and the *advaita* *synthesis*; the *Parliament* *of* *the* *World's* *Religions* (Chicago, 1893); the *Ramakrishna* *Mission* (1897) and the *social* *service* *model*; the *Neo*-*Vedanta* *and* *the* *"world* *religion"* *claim*.
5. **The *Theosophical* *Society* (1875)** — Blavatsky, *Isis* *Unveiled* (1877), *The* *Secret* *Doctrine* (1888), the *Olcott* *and* *Besant* *Hindu* *revival* (the *Theosophical* *Society*'s *Adyar* *headquarters*, the *Krishnamurti* *affair*).

### Chapter 48 — Globalization, Diaspora, and the Spiritual Marketplace (pp. 899–916)

1. **The *global* *migration* *of* *religions*** — the *Hindu* *diaspora* (the *Caribbean*, *East* *Africa*, the *British* *Hindu* *community*, the *Hare* *Krishna* *movement*), the *Muslim* *diaspora* *in* *Europe* (the *mosque* *and* *the* *headscarf* *debates*), the *Buddhist* *globalization* (the *Zen* *and* *Tibetan* *Buddhism* *in* *the* *West*), the *Pentecostal* *and* *Evangelical* *explosion* *in* *the* *Global* *South* (the *Latin* *American* *Pentecostals*, the *Nigerian* *mega-churches*, the *Korean* *church* *growth*).
2. **The *New* *Age* *and* *the* *"spiritual* *but* *not* *religious"*** — the *California* *counterculture* (1960s–70s), the *human* *potential* *movement*, *transpersonal* *psychology*, the *New* *Age* *publishing* *boom* (the *Neale* *Donald* *Walsch*, *The* *Celestine* *Prophecy*), the *"spirituality* *industry"* *and* *the* *"wellness"* *economy*; the *Yoga* *and* *mindfulness* *complex* (the *secularization* *of* *Buddhist* *meditation*, *McMindfulness*).
3. **The *mega-church* *and* *the* *digital* *congregation*** — the *megachurch* *phenomenon* (the *Saddleback*, *Lakewood*, *Hillsong*), the *"prosperity* *gospel"* (the *Word* *of* *Faith* *movement*), the *televangelism* *and* *the* *Internet* *of* *Religion* (the *online* *ritual*, the *AI* *chatbot* *confessors*, the *digital* *darshan*), the *podcast* *and* *the* *religious* *influencer*.
4. **New *religious* *movements* *and* *the* *cult* *panic*** — the *Scientology* (L. Ron Hubbard, 1952), the *Unification* *Church* (Sun Myung Moon, 1954), the *Wicca* *and* *modern* *Paganism* (Gerald Gardner, 1954; the *Goddess* *movement*), the *UFO* *religions* (the *Raëlians*, 1974; *Heaven's* *Gate*, 1997), the *Soka* *Gakkai* *International*, the *Falun* *Gong* (1992).
5. **The *"nones"* *and* *the* *unaffiliated*** — the *rise* *of* *the* *religiously* *unaffiliated* (c. 1.1 *billion* *globally*), the *atheist* *and* *secular* *movements*, the *"post-Christian"* *West* *and* *the* *"secular* *age"* *thesis*, the *"religious* *economy"* *model* *and* *its* *limits*.
6. **Interfaith *and* *intrafaith* *dialogue*** — the *World* *Parliament* *of* *Religions* (1993), the *Pontifical* *Council* *for* *Interreligious* *Dialogue*, the *"Scriptural* *Reasoning"* *movement*, the *joint* *statements* (the *"A* *Common* *Word* *Between* *Us* *and* *You"*, 2007), the *interfaith* *as* *a* *modern* *invention*.

### Chapter 49 — Religion, Violence, and Peacebuilding (pp. 917–934)

**Thesis.** Religion is neither inherently violent nor inherently peaceful; it is a language in which both are articulated, and the historian's task is to explain when and why each dominates.

1. **The *theoretical* *debate*** — the *"religion* *causes* *violence"* *thesis* (Hitchens, Harris), the *"religion* *is* *the* *mask"* *thesis* (William Cavanaugh's *The* *Myth* *of* *Religious* *Violence*, 2009), the *"ambivalence* *of* *the* *sacred"* (R. Scott Appleby, 2000), the *"religious* *war"* *as* *a* *modern* *category*.
2. **The *history* *of* *religious* *violence*** — the *Crusades* (1095–1291), the *Wars* *of* *Religion* (1524–1648), the *Partition* *of* *India* (1947) *and* *the* *"communalism"* *question*, the *Bosnian* *and* *Rwandan* *wars*, the *9/11* *attacks* (2001) *and* *the* *"clash* *of* *civilizations"* *thesis* (Huntington, 1996), the *Israeli-Palestinian* *conflict* *and* *the* *"religious* *nationalism"* *framing*.
3. **Religion *and* *peace*** — the *Quaker* *tradition* *of* *nonviolence*, *Gandhi*'s *satyagraha*, *Martin* *Luther* *King* *Jr.*, *Thich* *Nhat* *Hanh*, the *Dalai* *Lama* *and* *the* *"Religion* *of* *the* *Heart"*, the *Assisi* *meetings* (1986, 2002, 2011), the *Catholic* *Just* *War* *tradition* *and* *its* *revision* (Pope Francis, 2017).
4. **The *theology* *of* *interfaith* *respect*** — the *"pluralist"* *theology* (John Hick), the *"exclusivist/inclusivist"* *taxonomy* (Alan Race), the *Küng*'s *Global* *Ethic* (1993), the *Hans* *Küng* *and* *the* *Parliament* *of* *the* *World's* *Religions*.
5. **Gender, *sexuality*, *and* *religious* *conflict*** — the *"gender* *wars"* *of* *the* *contemporary* *church* *and* *the* *"family* *values"* *discourse*, the *Islamic* *feminism* *debate*, the *LGBTQ* *and* *the* *religious* *liberty* *controversies*, the *"conversion"* *and* *the* *"love* *jihad"* *discourse* *in* *India*.
6. **Religion *and* *the* *environment*** — the *"green* *religion"* *and* *the* *Pope* *Francis* *Laudato* *Si'* (2015), the *Islamic* *Declaration* *on* *Global* *Climate* *Change* (2015), the *Buddhist* *environmentalism* (Joanna Macy), the *Indigenous* *water* *protectors* *and* *the* *Standing* *Rock* *movement*.

### Chapter 50 — The Future of Religion: Demography, Technology, and the Return of the Questions (pp. 935–952)

**Thesis.** The last chapter returns to the book's first question — what is religion? — and finds it more contested, more vital, and more consequential than ever.

1. **The *demographic* *picture*** — the *Pew* *Research* *projections* (2015–2060), the *rise* *of* *Islam* *and* *Christianity* *in* *the* *Global* *South*, the *Christian* *decline* *in* *Europe*, the *"unaffiliated"* *population*, the *religious* *composition* *of* *Africa* *and* *Asia* *in* *2100*.
2. **The *secularization* *debate* *today*** — the *European* *exception* *thesis* (Grace Davie's *"believing* *without* *belonging"*), the *American* *exception*, the *"existential* *security"* *theory* (Norris & Inglehart), the *"religious* *market"* *model* *and* *the* *"supply-side"* *critique*.
3. **Technology *and* *the* *sacred*** — the *digital* *temple*, *AI* *and* *the* *question* *of* *machine* *consciousness* *and* *the* *soul*, the *transhumanist* *"religion"* *and* *the* *Singularity* (Ray Kurzweil), the *"digital* *relic"* *and* *the* *NFT* *sacred* *art*, the *metaverse* *as* *ritual* *space*.
4. **The *ecological* *crisis* *and* *the* *new* *cosmologies*** — the *"Anthropocene"* *and* *the* *theological* *response*, the *Gaia* *hypothesis* *and* *the* *Earth* *system* *science*, the *Indigenous* *ecological* *knowledge* *and* *the* *"Rights* *of* *Nature"* *movement*, the *climate* *grief* *and* *the* *"eco-theology"*.
5. **The *return* *of* *the* *questions*** — *Who* *are* *we*? *What* *is* *suffering*? *What* *is* *death*? *What* *do* *we* *owe* *each* *other*? — the *book* *returns* *to* *the* *Qafzeh* *burial*, *the* *Gītā*, *the* *Shema*, *the* *Sūra* *al-Fātiḥa*, *the* *Dhammapada*, *the* *Zhuangzi*, *the* *Phaedo*; the *common* *grammar* *of* *the* *human* *religious* *imagination*.
6. **A *historian's* *closing* *argument*** — the *duty* *of* *the* *historian* *to* *the* *faithful* *and* *the* *skeptic* *alike*; the *difference* *between* *understanding* *a* *tradition* *and* *believing* *it*; the *future* *of* *the* *discipline* *of* *religious* *studies*.

### Epilogue: A Historian's Meditation on Faith and Evidence (pp. 953–962)

A personal, reflective essay (10 pages) returning to the Qafzeh burials, the Lion-Man, and the *Shema*. The historian's three tasks: to take the sacred seriously as a human fact, to resist every form of triumphalism, and to leave the reader free. The final image: the "flower burial" at Shanidar — the oldest evidence we have of a human being treating a dead body as more than a body.

## VII. The Book's Five Cross-Cutting Thematic Threads (introduced in the Introduction; indexed in Appendix B)

1. **Authority and the body of the expert** — from the Mesopotamian *bārû* to the rabbi, the *mufti*, the bishop, the guru, the pastor, the "nones."
2. **Ritual as technology** — the argument that ritual is not "belief expressed" but *work done*: rain-making, healing, mourning, crowning, exorcising.
3. **Text, canon, and the invention of authority** — how the writing down of an oral tradition changed it (the *Veda*, the *Tipiṭaka*, the *Mishnah*, the *Quran*, the *Book of Mormon*).
4. **Religion, power, and violence** — the *Pax Deorum* to the *Wars of Religion* to 9/11; the "ambivalence of the sacred."
5. **The afterlife of the dead** — the oldest evidence of religion is a burial; the most persistent religious question is what happens after death; every chapter returns to this.
